Scoring Methodology

Scoring engine version: 50
Last updated: September 8, 2026

This page describes exactly how Underneath turns a product label into a number from 0 to 100. It is published so that anyone, including the brand being scored, can take the data on a package and arrive at the same figure the app shows.

Every rule that can change a score is listed here. If a score cannot be reproduced from this page and the product's own label data, that is a defect worth reporting.

1. The three axes

Each product is scored on three independent axes, each starting at 100 and losing points.

Two rules hold across all three. Everything is measured per 100 g or per 100 ml, never per serving, so a small pack cannot hide behind a small serving size. And nothing is scored against a category average, so a biscuit is never rewarded for being a better biscuit.

Additives

Every additive named on the label is matched against a reference table, keyed by E number and by name, and its penalty is subtracted from 100. The base scale follows regulatory standing.

PenaltyStatus
0Approved with no outstanding concern.
2An acceptable daily intake has been set.
5Under active safety re-evaluation.
8Banned in at least one jurisdiction. Classed as high risk.

A second scale covers substances approved everywhere but which exist to make cheap industrial food work: high fructose corn syrup 18 points, artificial flavours 15, maltodextrin 12, caramel colour 12, modified food starch 10, soy protein isolate and hydrolysed vegetable protein 8 each.

An additive that is present but not named, declared only by its functional class such as "emulsifier" or "sweetener", carries 5 points for the non-disclosure itself. It is not assumed to be the worst case, because an undeclared emulsifier may well be lecithin.

However long the list runs, this axis cannot fall below 40. Its purpose is to separate a formulation from a food, not to keep subtracting once that is established. A single additive banned somewhere in the world is the exception: it caps the axis at 49 whatever else the label shows.

Processing

Based on the NOVA framework, which sorts food by degree of industrial processing rather than by nutrient content. The group is taken from the product record where that is reliable and computed from the ingredient list where it is not.

PenaltyNOVA group
01, unprocessed or minimally processed. Fruit, plain oats, milk.
102, culinary ingredients. Oil, butter, salt, sugar.
303, processed food. Bread, cheese, tinned vegetables.
45 to 804, ultra-processed formulations, graded below.

NOVA 4 is graded rather than treated as one band, because a formulation built out of maltodextrin and modified starch is not the same product as a yoghurt containing one natural flavouring. Grading counts industrial markers, meaning ingredients that exist only because of industrial food production.

  • Three or more hard markers: 80 points, leaving 20.
  • One or two hard markers: 70 points, leaving 30.
  • Soft markers only: 45 points, leaving 55. Soft markers are the borderline declarations, meaning natural flavourings, stevia, and bare functional class words where the label never names the substance. They are enough to place a product in NOVA 4 but not enough to treat it as a full industrial formulation.

Organic certification softens the processing penalty, but only where the product carries no high risk additive: 5 points at NOVA 2, 10 at NOVA 3, 15 at NOVA 4, and nothing at NOVA 1 where there is no penalty to soften. It appears as its own line on the receipt so the underlying penalty stays visible rather than being quietly absorbed.

Plain water is forced to NOVA 1 before anything else is considered, because food databases routinely file bottled water under soft drinks. Coconut water, tonic water and flavoured water are excluded from that override.

Macros

Computed from the declared nutrition panel only. Nothing on this axis is assumed. If the panel contradicts itself, for example if the declared macronutrients could not fit in 100 grams or could not produce the declared calories, the panel is rejected and the axis returns no value rather than a guess.

  • Sodium. 30 points above 800 mg per 100 g, 18 above 400 mg, 8 above 120 mg.
  • Sugar. Judged on the share of the product's calories that come from sugar, not on grams alone, because a gram threshold punishes fruit and lets a low calorie syrup through. 45 points above a 75 percent share, 35 above 50 percent, 25 above 30 percent, 12 above 15 percent.
  • Trans fat. A flat 20 points for any detectable trans fat, or any partially hydrogenated oil on the list.
  • Saturated fat. 30 points above 10 g per 100 g, 18 above 5 g, 8 above 2 g. Waived where unsaturated fat is at least double the saturated fat, which is what protects olive oil, avocado and nuts. Deep fried products never receive that waiver, because the fat they carry is oxidised frying oil.
  • Calorie density. Up to 5 points to 50 kcal per 100 g, 15 to 200, 25 to 400, 35 above 400. Protein cancels up to 40 percent of it, fibre up to 35 percent, unsaturated fat up to 25 percent, and 15 g of protein or more per 100 g skips it outright.
  • Points returned. Fibre at 3 g or more per 100 g returns 5 points. Protein at 5 g or more returns 5 points.

Four guards keep the sugar calculation honest. A product carrying 5 g of sugar or less can never lose more than 12 points, and one carrying 10 g or less never more than 25. A product carrying more than 15 g loses at least 12 and one carrying more than 25 g loses at least 25, whatever the share works out to. A clean label product sweetened only with honey, maple syrup, molasses or dates has its sugar penalty halved. Whole food can never lose more than 12 points for sugar, because fruit is not a soft drink.

Individual ingredients may carry their own nutritional caveat, drawn from a reference set of about 140 entries, of which processed meat is the clearest example. Those adjustments are only ever negative, are counted once per ingredient, and their combined total is capped at 20 points so no axis is decided by them alone. A caveat may only charge points for something the label states. Whether a farm used a pesticide or an antibiotic is not on the label, so those notes are shown and cost nothing.

2. The weighting

When all three axes have data, the blend is:

blend = (additives × 0.30) + (processing × 0.30) + (macros × 0.40)

Macros carries the largest single weight because most of the measured harm from ultra-processed food runs through energy density, low satiety and low fibre. Additives carry less weight than they once did, because the evidence concerns a small set of compounds rather than additives in general. Genuinely harmful additives are handled by the hard cap at 49 rather than by weight, which is a sharper instrument than a heavier weighting.

This weighting changed in August 2026. See the version history at the end of this page.

3. The ultra-processed anchor

The blend alone is not the final number. For a product classified NOVA 4 that carries either at least one hard processing marker or a high risk additive, the blend is anchored to the processing axis:

score = round(processing × blend / 100)

This is the single rule with the largest effect on a low score, so it is set out in full.

Why it exists

Without it, a heavily processed product can average its way to a respectable number on the strength of a good nutrition panel. Reformulating a product to hit better macro targets while leaving it just as far from whole food is exactly the move the anchor is designed not to reward.

Worked example

A product scoring 40 on additives, 20 on processing and 77 on macros, classified NOVA 4 with three hard markers:

blend = (40 × 0.30) + (20 × 0.30) + (77 × 0.40) = 48.8
score = round(20 × 48.8 / 100) = 10

The same product without the NOVA 4 classification would score 49.

When the anchor does not apply

  • Clean label NOVA 4. A product pushed into NOVA 4 only by soft markers, with no high risk additive, escapes the anchor and is capped at 85 instead.
  • Category guesses. If the NOVA 4 classification came from the product's category rather than from markers seen on the package, the anchor does not apply. Nothing was read off that label to justify it.

4. Caps

A cap is a ceiling, never a deduction. Caps exist so that missing or untrustworthy data can never inflate a score. Where more than one applies, the lowest wins.

CapApplies when
49Any additive with a penalty worse than 5 points. Caps the additives axis, not the final score.
No scoreOnly one of the three axes had usable data. The internal ceiling is 49, which displays as Poor, so the number is withheld rather than shown: an absence of data is not a finding about the food.
74Only two of the three axes had usable data.
74The nutrition panel is missing sugar or sodium.
No scoreEvery scored axis came from a different barcode's record, matched on product name. Nothing in that record was read off the package in the reader's hand.
85Clean label NOVA 4, described above.
85The ingredient list was estimated rather than read, described in section 6.
70NOVA 3 or above, at least 450 kcal per 100 g, and low protein and fibre per calorie.
60, or 65 organicThe above, plus at least 28 g of fat per 100 g, taken as evidence of frying.
45, or 50 organicThe above, where the product category is a deep fried one such as crisps, corn chips or fries.
35A processed drink, NOVA 3 or above, carrying 8 g of sugar or more per 100 ml.
50A processed drink, NOVA 3 or above, carrying 5 g of sugar or more per 100 ml.
3Any alcoholic drink.

The two sugary drink caps are restricted to processed drinks so that milk, whose lactose runs to about 4.8 g per 100 ml, and juice that is only fruit, are never caught by them. Sugar that was already in the food is not treated as sugar that was added to it.

Alcohol is a Group 1 carcinogen under IARC with no established safe intake. The classification covers alcoholic beverages as a class and draws no distinction between a spirit, a wine and a beer, so neither does this cap. One number applies to all of them.

5. Verdict words

ScoreVerdict
80 to 100Good
50 to 79Moderate
0 to 49Poor

These words describe the output of this methodology. They are not a clinical or dietary assessment of a product, and a high score means close to whole food, not low calorie or suitable for any particular person.

6. Where the data comes from

  • Product data comes from Open Food Facts, a community maintained open database, supplemented by retailer product data in some markets.
  • Additive penalties and sources come from a reference table maintained by Underneath, each row carrying a citation shown in the app.
  • Label reading. Where a barcode is not in the database, the ingredient list is read from a photograph of the label.
  • Estimated ingredients. Where a product is known by name and category but has no ingredient list at all, a typical list may be estimated in order to give any answer. Estimated scores are labelled as such in the app, are capped at 85, and never raise an additive alert.

Product data can be wrong, out of date, or entered for a different market's version of the same package. The label on the product is always the authority.

A declared nutrition figure is an average, not a measurement of the pack in your hand. EU law requires it to be an average and allows a tolerance around it, which for sugars and fat in an ordinary food runs to about 2 g per 100 g at low levels and about 20 per cent at higher ones. Every threshold in section 4 reads the declared number and nothing else. Where two products sit either side of a threshold by less than that tolerance, the gap between their scores is not evidence that one is better than the other.

7. When no score is shown

Underneath shows no number at all, rather than a low one, when:

  • The ingredient list cannot be recognised well enough to score. A list that cannot be read is not evidence of a bad product.
  • No ingredient data exists and none could be estimated.
  • A score is under review following a challenge, or has been suspended because a defect was found. In that case the ingredients and the breakdown still display, but no number does. Section 9 sets out when this happens and how it ends.

8. Challenging a score

Anyone may challenge a score. There is no standing requirement and no need to be the brand. A shopper who thinks a number is wrong raises it the same way a manufacturer does, and it is examined the same way.

What to send

A score can only be examined properly if the product can be identified and its label read, so a challenge needs:

  • the barcode;
  • the country the product was bought in, because the same barcode carries different formulations in different markets;
  • a photograph of the ingredient list and the nutrition panel as printed on the pack sold in that market;
  • which part of the score is disputed: the number itself, a specific additive match, the processing classification, a cap, or a line of the breakdown.

A challenge missing some of these is not rejected. It is acknowledged, and the missing part is requested once.

What happens, and by when

StageTime
Written acknowledgement, giving a reference for the file3 working days
Substantive answer20 working days
If the examination will take longer, notice of that with a reason and a dateBefore day 20

The substantive answer contains the full working: the product data used and where it came from, every additive matched and the reference row that matched it, the NOVA classification and what drove it, every cap that applied, and the arithmetic from the three pillar scores to the number displayed. That is provided whether or not the challenge succeeds.

What is recorded

One file per challenge, holding the date received and from whom, the barcode and market, what was disputed, the score and engine version in force at the time, the label data relied on, whether the number was suspended and when, the finding, what changed as a result, and the date the file closed. Kept for six years from closure.

A correction with no record of why it happened is indistinguishable from a number that moved on its own, which is why the record exists.

What can and cannot be reproduced later

The scoring rules are kept permanently, at every version they have ever had, along with the reason for every change to an additive's points, its stated ground or its citation. So the question of why a given ingredient cost a given number of points on a given date is answerable for any date.

An individual score is a different matter and it would be false to claim otherwise. The stored data behind a particular scan is kept for 45 days, and product data can change under a barcode at any time because the source database is community maintained. What survives without limit is the arithmetic, not the inputs. A challenge raised later is therefore answered by recomputing the score from the label data supplied with it, which is exactly what the app does on every scan.

9. Suspending and correcting a score

When a number is suspended

A displayed number is withheld when a challenge is raised that is not obviously unfounded and goes to the substance of the score, or when a defect is found that could have produced a wrong number for a class of products, whether or not anyone complained.

Not obviously unfounded is deliberately a low bar. Disagreeing with the result is not enough. Pointing at a specific input, match, classification or step is.

While a score is suspended the ingredients and the breakdown still display and the number does not. No substitute number is shown, no estimate replaces it, and nothing implies the product scored badly. It is the same behaviour as every other case in section 7 where a number is withheld rather than guessed.

How a suspension ends

Only in one of two ways, and both are recorded. Either the score is corrected and the corrected number is displayed, or the original score is confirmed with reasons given to whoever raised it and the original number is restored. A suspension is never lifted silently, and never lifted merely because time passed.

Three kinds of wrong

  • Wrong product data. The label says something the database did not. The data is refreshed and the score recomputes on the next scan. No rule changes.
  • Wrong rule application. The rules were right and were applied wrongly. A code fix, with a test that fails before it and passes after.
  • Wrong rule. The penalty, the stated ground or the citation does not hold. The rule itself changes, carrying a written record of what was wrong and what the evidence actually supports, and this page and its version history are updated in the same pass.

A correction of the third kind changes every product carrying that ingredient, not only the one that was disputed. That is the point. The rule was wrong, so every score that relied on it was wrong.

10. Published comparisons

The scoring system is published and open to challenge. A video or a post is not covered by any of that unless the same discipline is applied to it, and a claim made in a caption can do as much damage as a number in the app. Every one of the following has to hold before anything naming another brand's product is published, and the check is recorded alongside it.

  • Every factual claim traces to a source recorded before publication. A claim about what is in a product traces to that product's own current label. A claim about an authority's view names the authority, the document and its date.
  • No claim that a named product is unsafe, harmful or unlawful unless a named authority has said so. Where one has, it is quoted as what that authority said, on what date, in which jurisdiction, and not restated as a general fact.
  • A comparison is like for like: the same measure on both sides, per the same quantity, from current label data for both, with the date the data was read.
  • A comparison rests only on rules published on this page. Nothing is implied about a product that the published rules do not actually charge it for.
  • The comparison is verifiable by the person watching it, with barcodes or product names specific enough to buy and repeat.
  • No claim about how a product is made, sourced or farmed unless the label or a cited source says it. This is the same rule that removed the farming practice caveats from the scoring engine in version 50, applied to content.

Where a named brand or anyone else shows that a published claim is wrong or cannot be supported, the item comes down within 5 working days, and where the original had meaningful reach a correction is published rather than the item merely disappearing. An item is taken down when the claim cannot be supported. It is not taken down merely because a brand objects to being named, and the two are recorded differently.

11. Suggested alternatives

Nobody pays to appear here. No product, brand, retailer or other party pays to be suggested as an alternative, to be ranked higher among the suggestions, or to be kept out of them. There is no advertising placement, no affiliate link and no sponsorship in this feature. The rule below is the whole rule.

After a product is scored, the app may suggest better products in the same category. A recommendation is a stronger statement than a rating, so the rule is published in full.

  1. Category. Candidates come from the most specific category the scanned product carries that returns anything at all. Categories too broad to mean anything, such as "beverages and beverage preparations", are excluded outright. If nothing exists in the right category, nothing is shown. There is no topping up from a different category.
  2. Market. Where the country is known, candidates come only from that country. A product that cannot be bought is not an alternative.
  3. Shortlist. The search asks Open Food Facts for products graded A or B on Nutri-Score, and only A where the scanned product is not already Poor. At most 8 are kept. Nutri-Score is a third party grade and it decides one thing only: which 8 products are worth the cost of scoring. It does not enter the Underneath score, does not decide what is shown, and does not decide the order.
  4. Scoring. All 8 are scored by the engine described on this page, on the same rules and the same data as any other product.
  5. Filter. A candidate is shown only if it scores higher than the scanned product and either reaches 70 or beats it by at least 5 points. Where the scanned product has no score, a candidate must reach 50.
  6. Floor. Nothing scoring below 50 is ever suggested, under any condition. The app does not recommend a product it rates Poor.
  7. Order. Survivors are ordered by Underneath score, highest first, and at most 5 are displayed.

Because step 3 asks only for Nutri-Score A and B, a product this system would rate well but Nutri-Score rates C or worse is never scored and so never suggested. The two systems weigh different things, so this will happen. Scoring every product in a category on every scan is not affordable, and the honest consequence is that these are suggestions of better products found, not a claim to have found the best one available. The app does not say otherwise.

12. Product images

Photographs and packaging images shown next to a product are not Underneath's. They come from Open Food Facts, whose photographs are contributed by its community and licensed for reuse, and in some markets from retailer or manufacturer product listings, where the image is displayed to identify the product being discussed and nothing more. No image is presented as Underneath's own work and no ownership of any brand's packaging, artwork or trade mark is claimed.

A rights holder who does not want a particular image shown does not need to argue the point. Write to the address in section 8 identifying the image and the product, and it is removed within 5 working days, with no requirement to establish anything first. The product itself remains scannable and scored; only the image goes. Where an image was wrongly attributed or wrongly sourced, that is recorded the same way a scoring correction is.

13. Version history

September 8, 2026, third entry

Two changes about what a thin record is allowed to say.

A product scored on only one axis no longer shows a number. The ceiling in that case is 49, and 49 displays as Poor, so a record too thin to judge reached the reader in the words reserved for a product that scored badly on evidence. The ceiling still governs the arithmetic. The number is now withheld, the product reads N/A, and the receipt says that only one of the three axes could be scored. No product got worse. Some products that read Poor now read as unscored, which is what they were.

An undeclared protein or fibre row no longer counts as zero. The test that decides which energy density cap a product falls to divides protein and fibre by calories. It treated a row the panel never printed as a zero, so a label that simply said less scored worse than a fuller one. Both figures must now be declared before that test can apply at all. A few energy dense products with incomplete panels gain points.

September 8, 2026, second entry

Four scoring changes, each of which moves real numbers.

Alcohol is now one cap, not four. The cap was tiered by drink type: 5 for clear spirits, 4 for wine, 3 for beer and aged spirits, 1 for liqueurs and alcopops. The reason given for all four was the IARC Group 1 classification, which covers alcoholic beverages as a class and makes no such distinction. A tiered penalty on an untiered ground published, in a number, that a gin is better for you than a beer. All alcoholic drinks now cap at 3. Spirits and wine go down, liqueurs and cocktails go up.

Saturated fat, sodium and calorie density are no longer charged twice. Those figures are printed on the pack and charged against the product's own numbers by the macros axis. Fifteen ingredient caveats repeated the same point using a typical figure instead, so a butter was charged for its fat and then charged again for being butter. Those caveats are still shown. They now cost points only where the panel did not declare the figure, or where a rule waived the panel penalty. Butter, cheese, cream, milk, yoghurt and similar products gain a few points.

Honey, syrups and fruit juice no longer get the intrinsic sugar allowance. Unprocessed products have their sugar penalty capped, because the sugar in an orange or in milk is not added sugar. Honey, syrup and juice are unprocessed by that classification and the WHO guideline the rule cites counts their sugars in full, naming them specifically. The cap is now withheld from them. Those products lose points.

The additive cap line now says which axis it capped. It read "Score capped at 49", which is a ceiling on the additives axis rather than on the final score. It now reads "Additives score capped at 49". No score moved.

Section 6 also now states that a declared nutrition figure is a legal average with a tolerance around it, and that a small gap between two products across a threshold is not evidence of a real difference.

September 8, 2026

Published two things that were previously only internal. Section 11 sets out how suggested alternatives are chosen, including that the shortlist is drawn using Nutri-Score, a third party grade, and that this decides only which products get scored and never what is shown or in what order. It also states in terms that no one pays to appear there. Section 12 sets out where product images come from and how a rights holder has one removed. Neither changes a score.

Published the procedure around the scores rather than the scores themselves: what a challenge has to contain and how long an answer takes, what is recorded and for how long, when a number is suspended and how a suspension ends, and the rules a published comparison naming another brand has to satisfy. Sections 8, 9 and 10 above. No rule changed and no score changed. What changed is that the process is now written down and can be held to.

Section 8 also states plainly what can and cannot be reproduced after the fact. The rules are kept permanently. The stored data behind an individual scan is not, and a late challenge is answered by recomputing from the label rather than from an archived number.

Version 50, September 2026

Ingredient caveats that describe farming practice rather than label content no longer cost points. Conventional dairy and conventional poultry previously carried a small deduction for possible pesticide, hormone or antibiotic residues. None of that is stated on a label, so no product could be shown why it had lost the points. Those notes are still displayed, and now cost nothing. Conventional dairy products gain about 1 point. Plain chicken and turkey lose their only such caveat, so they now carry no ingredient deduction at all.

Version 49, September 2026

Published this page. Corrected the description of the weighting on the home page, which still read 50 percent additives, 30 percent processing and 20 percent macros. The engine had used 30 / 30 / 40 since August 2026 and the figures on the home page were not updated at the time. The app itself displayed the correct weighting throughout. No score changed as a result of this correction; only the published description of it did.

Also in this version: the ultra-processed anchor and the full cap list are published for the first time.

August 2026

Weighting changed from 50 percent additives, 30 percent processing and 20 percent macros to 30 / 30 / 40, on the view that the additives axis should not outrank measured nutrition, and that specific harmful additives are better handled by a hard cap than by a heavier weight across every additive. Processing was unchanged.

July 2026

Weighting moved from 40 percent additives, 30 percent processing and 30 percent macros to 50 / 30 / 20. This is the weighting the home page described until the September correction above.

Earlier

Scores are recomputed from live data on every scan, so a change to this methodology takes effect immediately. No score is served from a stale calculation.
